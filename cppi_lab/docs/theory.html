§1.1CPPI Invariant Wealth Floor & Safety Buffer Derivation

↔ Technical §1.1 · Floor & Safety Buffer State Kernel

The account starts with $N_0$ BTC and $c_0$ USD. The user names a price $P_{\text{liq}}$ below which the account must hold no Bitcoin. Valuing the initial coins at that price defines the amount of wealth the strategy promises never to fall below, the hard floor

$$F = N_0 \cdot P_{\text{liq}} + c_0 .$$

Wealth is measured net of the cost of leaving

Selling in a hurry costs the taker fee $f_{\text{taker}}$ and some slippage $\delta_{\text{slip}}$. Their sum $\gamma = f_{\text{taker}} + \delta_{\text{slip}}$ is the emergency exit friction. A position of $n_t$ BTC and $c_t$ USD is therefore worth, if it had to be closed at price $P$,

$$V_t(P) = c_t + n_t \, P \, (1-\gamma).$$

This liquidation value, not the mark-to-market value, is what the floor constrains: the invariant of the whole strategy is $V_t \ge F$ for every bar $t$.

Why the exit cannot wait for $P_{\text{liq}}$

Suppose the account still holds its initial coins and sells only when the market prints $P_{\text{liq}}$. The proceeds are $N_0 P_{\text{liq}}(1-\gamma) + c_0 = F - \gamma N_0 P_{\text{liq}}$, which is below the floor by exactly the friction. The exit has to be triggered earlier, at a price $P_{\text{trig}} > P_{\text{liq}}$ high enough for two things to hold at once.

  1. The proceeds cover the floor. $c_t + n_t P_{\text{trig}}(1-\gamma) \ge F$, that is $P_{\text{trig}} \ge \dfrac{F - c_t}{n_t (1-\gamma)}$.
  2. The realised price is not below $P_{\text{liq}}$. After friction the sale is done at $P_{\text{trig}}(1-\gamma)$, so $P_{\text{trig}} \ge \dfrac{P_{\text{liq}}}{1-\gamma}$.

The lowest price satisfying both is the liquidation level plus safety buffer:

$$P_{\text{floor},t} = \max\!\left(\frac{P_{\text{liq}}}{1-\gamma},\; \frac{\max(0,\; F - c_t)}{n_t\,(1-\gamma)}\right) \qquad (n_t > 0).$$

The distance $\Delta P_{\text{buf},t} = P_{\text{floor},t} - P_{\text{liq}}$ is the safety buffer. It is never smaller than its baseline

$$\Delta P_{\text{buf},0} = P_{\text{liq}} \cdot \frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma},$$

which at the defaults ($P_{\text{liq}} = 60{,}000$, $\gamma = 0.40\,\%$) is $240.96$ USD: the trigger sits at $60{,}240.96$.

Which term binds

Define the locked-in slack $s_t = c_t + n_t P_{\text{liq}} - F$: what would be left above the floor if every remaining coin were sold at a realised price of exactly $P_{\text{liq}}$. Rearranging the two terms of the maximum shows that the second one is the larger exactly when $s_t < 0$. At the start $s_0 = 0$ and both terms coincide. Coins sold above $P_{\text{liq}}$ make $s_t$ positive and leave the trigger at its baseline; coins bought back above the price they were sold at make it negative, and the trigger rises (see §1.2).

Proposition 1 · exit at the trigger restores the floor

If the emergency sale of all $n_t$ coins is executed at the trigger, the account ends with $c' = c_t + n_t P_{\text{floor},t}(1-\gamma) \ge F$ and realised price $P_{\text{floor},t}(1-\gamma) \ge P_{\text{liq}}$.

Proof. By construction $P_{\text{floor},t}$ is at least each term of the maximum. The second term gives $n_t P_{\text{floor},t}(1-\gamma) \ge F - c_t$; the first gives $P_{\text{floor},t}(1-\gamma) \ge P_{\text{liq}}$. ∎

Proposition 1 is only half of the guarantee: it says the exit is sufficient, not that the account is always in a state from which the exit is still possible. That requires every trade the strategy makes to leave the trigger below the market, which is the subject of §5.1.

§1.2Whipsaw Decay as Dynamic Put Replication Cost

↔ Technical §1.2 · Cushion & Recoverable BTC Accounting

A floor on wealth is a put option. The strategy does not buy one; it manufactures one by trading: it sells as the price falls toward the floor and buys back as it recovers. Any rule of that shape sells low and buys high whenever the market reverses, and the money lost that way is the premium of the synthetic put. It is paid in arrears, and its size depends on how much the price oscillated, not on where it ended.

The cost of one round trip

Sell $q$ BTC at price $P_s$ with friction rate $\varphi_s$, then buy the same quantity back at $P_b$ with friction $\varphi_b$. Cash changes by

$$\Delta c = q\,P_s\,(1-\varphi_s) - q\,P_b\,(1+\varphi_b) = -\,q\,\underbrace{(P_b - P_s)}_{\text{price whipsaw}} \;-\; q\,\underbrace{(\varphi_s P_s + \varphi_b P_b)}_{\text{fees and slippage}} .$$

If the market kept falling after the sale, $P_b < P_s$ and the first term is a gain: the insurance paid out. If it reversed, both terms are losses. The KPI Fees + Slippage reports the second term exactly; the first shows up as the gap between the equity curve and a buy-and-hold line.

Recoverable inventory

The cleanest single measure of the damage is the number of coins the account could hold if it converted all its cash back at the current price,

$$n_{\max,t} = n_t + \frac{c_t}{P_t\,\big(1 + f_{\text{eff}}(\rho)\big)}, \qquad f_{\text{eff}}(\rho) = (1-\rho)\,f_{\text{maker}} + \rho\,f_{\text{taker}},$$

where $f_{\text{eff}}$ is the fee the router would pay on average at risk aversion $\rho$. A round trip at a loss lowers $n_{\max}$: the account can no longer afford the coins it started with.

Why whipsaw raises the trigger

Write $A = F - c_t$ for the amount the remaining coins must still raise. The second term of the trigger is $A / (n_t(1-\gamma))$.

Lemma · direction of the trigger

Selling $q$ coins for a net unit price $s$ lowers (or keeps) $A/n$ if and only if $s \ge A/n$. Buying $q$ coins at an all-in unit cost $a$ raises $A/n$ if and only if $a > A/n$.

Proof. After the sale the ratio is $(A - qs)/(n-q)$, and $(A-qs)\,n \le A\,(n-q) \iff s\,n \ge A$. After the purchase it is $(A+qa)/(n+q)$, and $(A+qa)\,n > A\,(n+q) \iff a\,n > A$. ∎

The strategy only sells above the trigger, where $s \ge A/n$ as long as the maker fee is smaller than $\gamma$, so selling never raises the trigger. It only buys above the trigger, where $a > A/n$, so every purchase raises the required level. While the locked-in slack of §1.1 is positive this is invisible, because the baseline term still binds. Once the slack has been spent, the trigger itself starts to climb: the dynamic whipsaw buffer expansion drawn as the orange stepped line in pane 1.

The cushion bounds the premium

The quantity being spent is the active risk cushion $C_t = \max(0, V_t - F)$. A purchase of $q$ coins at all-in cost $a$ changes the liquidation value at the same price by $-q\,(a - P(1-\gamma))$, the full round-trip friction, so no purchase can be larger than the cushion can pay for. CPPI-Lab enforces this with a rebuy cap: a buy is trimmed until the position it creates is still at or below the sell ceiling evaluated on the post-trade state (§2.1). Two consequences follow.

  • The premium is bounded: the strategy can lose its cushion and nothing more.
  • When the cushion is gone the cap is zero. The account sits in cash at the floor and cannot re-enter however far the market rallies. This cash lock is the defining weakness of every CPPI scheme, and the workbench makes it easy to see how quickly a noisy signal reaches it.

§2.1Dual-Envelope Control Law & Hysteresis Deadband

↔ Technical §2.1 · Envelope Evaluation & Minimum Rebalance Quantum

The control law answers one question at every bar close: how many coins may the account hold? It answers with an interval rather than a number, and trades only when the holding leaves the interval.

Normalised distance

Let $P_{\text{ref}}$ be the price at which the user is content to be fully invested. The position of the market between the trigger and that reference is

$$d_t = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{P_t - P_{\text{floor},t}}{\max(P_{\text{ref}} - P_{\text{floor},t},\ 1)},\ 0,\ 1\right).$$

The straight line $n_{\max,t}\,d_t$ is the linear glidepath: nothing at the trigger, everything at the reference. The $\max(\cdot, 1)$ keeps the ratio defined when whipsaw has pushed the trigger up to the reference; the band is then one dollar wide.

The two edges

The sell ceiling bends below the glidepath when the decline signal fires:

$$n_{\text{ceil},t} = n_{\max,t}\; d_t\; \Big(1 - k_{\text{sell}}\,(1-d_t)\,S_{\text{down},t}\Big).$$

The rebuy floor rises toward the glidepath only when the growth signal says so:

$$n_{\text{floor},t} = n_{\max,t}\; d_t\; S_{\text{up},t}.$$
  • Complete sale at the floor. $d_t \to 0$ as $P_t \to P_{\text{floor},t}$, so $n_{\text{ceil},t} \to 0$ whatever the signals say.
  • Where the signal matters. $\partial n_{\text{ceil}} / \partial S_{\text{down}} = -k_{\text{sell}}\, n_{\max}\, d\,(1-d)$ is largest half-way down the band and vanishes at both ends. Near the reference there is little to protect; near the trigger the glidepath has already sold.
  • A well-ordered band. With $k_{\text{sell}} \le 1$ the veto gate of §4.1 gives $S_{\text{up}} \le 1 - S_{\text{down}} \le 1 - k_{\text{sell}}(1-d)S_{\text{down}}$, hence $n_{\text{floor}} \le n_{\text{ceil}}$ automatically. For larger gains the bracket of the ceiling is floored at zero and the rebuy floor is capped at the ceiling, so the band can shrink to a line but never invert.

Hysteresis

Nothing happens while $n_{\text{floor},t} \le n_t \le n_{\text{ceil},t}$. Outside the band an order is placed only if it is worth placing:

  • Sell $\Delta n = n_t - n_{\text{ceil},t}$ when $n_t > n_{\text{ceil},t}$ and $\Delta n \ge \Delta n_{\text{eff},t}$.
  • Buy $\Delta n = n_{\text{floor},t} - n_t$ when $n_t < n_{\text{floor},t}$, $\Delta n \ge \Delta n_{\min}$ and $S_{\text{down},t} < 0.25$.

The minimum quantum $\Delta n_{\min}$ is a share of $N_0$. For sells it is waived inside the last 250 USD above the trigger, $\Delta n_{\text{eff},t} = 0$ if $P_t \le P_{\text{floor},t} + 250$, because a position that may have to be closed in the next minute should not be kept alive by a rounding rule. The three mechanisms - the band, the quantum and the buy veto - are what stand between a minute-by-minute signal and a fee bill of the same frequency.

Stability at the instant of a trade

Proposition 2 · no trade lands outside its own envelope

After a sell to the ceiling, the holding is not above the ceiling recomputed on the post-trade state. After a buy, the same holds by construction.

Sketch. By the lemma of §1.2 a sale does not raise the trigger, so $d$ does not fall; $n_{\max}$ changes only by the friction of the sale. A purchase does raise the trigger, which lowers $d$ and with it the ceiling; without a limit the new holding could exceed the new ceiling and be sold again in the next bar. The rebuy cap chooses the largest quantity $q \le \Delta n$ with $n_t + q \le n_{\text{ceil}}'(q)$, where the prime denotes evaluation on the state after buying $q$. ∎

Since $n_{\text{ceil}}' > 0$ requires $d' > 0$, the cap also implies $P_t > P_{\text{floor}}'$: a purchase can lift the trigger, but never above the price it was made at. That inequality is what the floor invariant needs from the buy side.

§2.2Continuous Risk-Aversion ($\rho$) & Urgency Escalation Theory

↔ Technical §2.2 · Order Routing Engine (route_order_by_rho)

Once the envelope has decided to trade $\Delta n$, the order still has to be executed, and there are two ways to do it.

RouteCost per unit of notionalCertainty
Passive post_only maker order pegged at the close $f_{\text{maker}}$ (default 0.15 %) Fills in the next bar with probability $P_{\text{fill}}$ (§3.1)
Immediate taker order $\gamma = f_{\text{taker}} + \delta_{\text{slip}}$ (default 0.40 %) Always fills

The trade-off

A maker order saves $\gamma - f_{\text{maker}}$ of the notional when it fills. When it does not, the position the envelope wanted to shed is still there one bar later, and for a sell order it is still there precisely because the market moved away, downward. If $\mathbb{E}[\Delta P \mid \text{miss}]$ is the expected adverse move conditional on a miss, posting is worth it roughly while

$$P_{\text{fill}}\,(\gamma - f_{\text{maker}})\,P \;>\; (1 - P_{\text{fill}})\;\mathbb{E}[\Delta P \mid \text{miss}] .$$

The left side is small and fixed. The right side grows with volatility, with proximity to the trigger, where a miss can turn into an emergency exit, and with every miss already suffered. No single rule is right for every user, so the choice is exposed as one number, the risk aversion $\rho \in [0,1]$.

Execution urgency

Let $m$ be the number of consecutive bars on which the maker order of the current rebalance has missed. The urgency of the order is

$$u_t = \begin{cases} 1 - d_t\,(1 - S_{\text{down},t})\; 0.5^{\,m} & \text{selling},\\[4pt] S_{\text{up},t}\,\big(1 - 0.5^{\,m+1}\big) & \text{buying}. \end{cases}$$

A sell is calm only if three things are true together: the price is far from the trigger ($d$ near 1), the decline signal is quiet, and the order has not been missed before. Each miss halves what calm remains. A buy is never urgent on its own account; its urgency is the strength of the growth signal, discounted on the first attempt and approaching $S_{\text{up}}$ as misses accumulate.

The routing law

$$\theta_{\text{taker},t}(\rho) = \begin{cases} 0 & \rho = 0,\\ 1 & \rho = 1 \ \text{ or } \ u_t \ge 1-\rho,\\ \rho & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

$\theta\,\Delta n$ is sent as a taker order at once; $(1-\theta)\,\Delta n$ is posted as a maker order at the close and resolved in the next bar.

  • $\rho = 0$. Every rebalance above the trigger is passive. The only taker order the strategy ever sends is the emergency exit when $P_{\text{floor},t}$ is reached. The realised price of that exit lies inside the buffer $[P_{\text{liq}}, P_{\text{floor},t}]$, so $V_t \ge F$ still holds.
  • $\rho = 1$. Every rebalance is an immediate taker order. Nothing is left to chance and the Monte Carlo fan has zero width.
  • $0 < \rho < 1$. Calm orders are split, a fraction $\rho$ now and the rest passively. Urgent orders escalate to 100 % taker. For a sell the escalation condition $u_t \ge 1 - \rho$ reads $d_t(1-S_{\text{down},t})\,2^{-m} \le \rho$, so an order that starts calm escalates after $m^\star = \big\lceil \log_2\!\big(d_t(1-S_{\text{down},t})/\rho\big) \big\rceil$ misses: at most two at the default $\rho = 0.25$.
Maker dominance

The whole trade-off presupposes that a passive order is cheaper. If the user sets $f_{\text{maker}} \ge f_{\text{taker}} + \delta_{\text{slip}}$ it is dearer and uncertain, and no value of $\rho$ can justify it. The router then sends every order as a taker. The same rule keeps the lemma of §1.2 valid: a maker sale at a net price below the taker price could otherwise raise the trigger.

§3.1Stochastic Order-Book Liquidity & Pass-Through Theory

↔ Technical §3.1 · Stochastic Fill Sampler & PCG64 Seeding

A back-test that fills every limit order the bar touched is flattering itself. A resting order sits in a queue behind everyone who posted at that price earlier. When the price merely touches the level, only the front of the queue trades; when it sweeps through quickly, the level can be consumed and abandoned before a small order near the back is reached. This is liquidity pass-through, and it is most likely exactly when a protective sell order is most needed.

A maker order of size $q$ posted at $P_{\text{lim}} = C_{t-1}$ is resolved against bar $t$. Let $\text{ATR}_t$ be the average true range of the decline signal, floored at 1 USD; it turns dollar distances into multiples of a typical one-minute move.

Factor 1 · queue penetration

The order can only fill if the market trades through its price. The depth of that excursion is $\Delta P_{\text{pen},t} = H_t - P_{\text{lim}}$ for a sell and $P_{\text{lim}} - L_t$ for a buy. If it is not positive, the probability is zero. Otherwise model the volume that trades beyond the limit as arriving at a rate proportional to the depth of the excursion; the chance that at least the queue ahead of the order is consumed is then the complement of an exponential,

$$\Phi_{\text{pen},t} = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\lambda_{\text{liq}}\,\frac{\Delta P_{\text{pen},t}}{\text{ATR}_t}\right).$$

$\lambda_{\text{liq}}$ is the depth factor: at the default 2.5 an excursion of one ATR clears the queue with probability 0.92, and one of a quarter ATR with probability 0.46.

Factor 2 · adverse velocity

A bar that closed far from its open in the direction away from the order spent little time near the limit. The adverse move is $v_{\text{adv},t} = \max(0, O_t - C_t)/\text{ATR}_t$ for a sell and $\max(0, C_t - O_t)/\text{ATR}_t$ for a buy, and the time available decays exponentially with it:

$$\Phi_{\text{vel},t} = \exp\!\big(-\beta_{\text{vel}}\; v_{\text{adv},t}\big).$$

This is the term that makes a resting sell fail in a crash: a red bar two ATR long leaves $e^{-3.6} \approx 0.03$ of the probability at the default $\beta_{\text{vel}} = 1.8$.

Factor 3 · counter-party absorption

A resting sell is filled by buyers who cross the spread. Let $V_{\text{opp},t}$ be the taker volume on the opposite side during the bar (buyer-taker volume for a sell, seller-taker volume for a buy) and take one per cent of it as the share a single price level can expect. The probability that an order of size $q$ is absorbed is a Hill function of the ratio,

$$\Phi_{\text{size},t} = \frac{1}{1 + \left(\dfrac{q}{\max(0.01\,V_{\text{opp},t},\ 10^{-8})}\right)^{\alpha_{\text{size}}}} ,$$

one half when the order equals the absorbable volume, falling faster for larger $\alpha_{\text{size}}$.

The coin flip

$$P_{\text{fill},t} = \operatorname{clip}\big(\Phi_{\text{pen},t}\,\Phi_{\text{vel},t}\,\Phi_{\text{size},t},\ 0,\ 1\big), \qquad \text{fill} \iff U_t \le P_{\text{fill},t},\quad U_t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1).$$

A fill executes at the limit and pays $f_{\text{maker}}$. A miss leaves the account unchanged, is logged as a missed order with its $P_{\text{fill}}$ and $U$, and raises the miss counter that drives the urgency of §2.2.

Common random numbers

The variates are generated once from the seed $s_{\text{rng}}$ and indexed by bar, not drawn on demand. Two back-tests with the same seed therefore face the same luck on every bar, even if one of them trades more often. Differences between them are differences of the parameters, not of the dice, which is what a sensitivity test needs.

The ensemble

One seed is one possible history of the queue. Running $M = 100$ seeds gives a distribution of outcomes for the same prices and the same parameters; the band between its 5th and 95th percentiles is the Monte Carlo liquidity fan. Its width is the part of the result that is luck of the fill, and the fan is the right place to check that the floor holds for every seed rather than for the one on screen.

§4.1Asymmetric Decline ($S_{\text{down}}$) & Growth ($S_{\text{up}}$) Microstructure Signals

↔ Technical §4.1 · Vectorized Signal Pipeline

Two numbers between 0 and 1 modulate the envelope. They are deliberately not mirror images: leaving late is expensive in a way that returning late is not, so the decline signal is fast and reads the order flow, while the growth signal is slow, asks for confirmation, and can be vetoed.

Building blocks

The true range of a bar and the exponential average with span $W$ are

$$\text{TR}_t = \max\big(H_t - L_t,\ |H_t - C_{t-1}|,\ |L_t - C_{t-1}|\big),\qquad \mathcal{E}_W(X)_t = \alpha_W X_t + (1-\alpha_W)\,\mathcal{E}_W(X)_{t-1},\quad \alpha_W = \frac{2}{W+1}.$$

Dividing a price difference by an average true range makes it dimensionless and comparable across volatility regimes; the range is floored at 1 USD so a dead market cannot manufacture a signal from noise.

Decline signal

Momentum. When the fast average of the close is below the slow one, the market has been falling faster recently than before:

$$M_{\text{down},t} = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{W_{s,d}}(C)_t - \mathcal{E}_{W_{f,d}}(C)_t}{1.5\,\max\big(\mathcal{E}_{W_{\text{atr},d}}(\text{TR})_t,\ 1\big)},\ 0,\ 1\right).$$

Flow imbalance. Price can drift on thin trading; aggression cannot be faked. With $\Sigma_{\text{sell}}$ and $\Sigma_{\text{buy}}$ the taker volumes summed over the last $W_{\text{imb},d}$ bars,

$$I_{\text{down},t} = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\Sigma_{\text{sell},t} - \Sigma_{\text{buy},t}}{0.40\,\max(\Sigma_{\text{sell},t} + \Sigma_{\text{buy},t},\ 10^{-8})},\ 0,\ 1\right),$$

which reaches 1 when sellers account for 70 % of the aggressive volume.

$$S_{\text{down},t} = \operatorname{clip}\big(w_{m,d}\,M_{\text{down},t} + (1-w_{m,d})\,I_{\text{down},t},\ 0,\ 1\big).$$

Growth signal

Trend. The same construction with slower windows and a wider normalisation, so it takes a larger and longer move to saturate:

$$M_{\text{up},t} = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{W_{f,u}}(C)_t - \mathcal{E}_{W_{s,u}}(C)_t}{2.0\,\max\big(\mathcal{E}_{W_{\text{atr},u}}(\text{TR})_t,\ 1\big)},\ 0,\ 1\right).$$

Breakout. A recovery that is still in the lower half of its recent range is a bounce, not a trend. With $H^{(W)}_t$ and $L^{(W)}_t$ the highest high and lowest low of the last $W_{\text{brk},u}$ bars, midpoint $M_t$ and half-span $R_t = \max(\tfrac12(H^{(W)}_t - L^{(W)}_t),\,1)$,

$$B_{\text{up},t} = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{C_t - M_t}{R_t},\ 0,\ 1\right).$$

Crash veto. The worst purchase is the one made half-way down a liquidation cascade. The gate scales the growth signal down as the decline signal rises and shuts it completely at $\tau_{\text{veto}}$:

$$G_{\text{veto},t} = \max\!\left(0,\ 1 - \frac{S_{\text{down},t}}{\tau_{\text{veto}}}\right),\qquad S_{\text{up},t} = \operatorname{clip}\big((0.5\,M_{\text{up},t} + 0.5\,B_{\text{up},t})\,G_{\text{veto},t},\ 0,\ 1\big).$$

What the signals cost

Both signals move every minute, and every move shifts an edge of the envelope. The hysteresis of §2.1 absorbs small shifts; large ones become orders. Shortening the windows buys earlier exits at the price of more round trips; the fee and missed-order KPIs show which side of that trade a setting is on.

§5.1Discrete-Time Execution Model, Event Ordering & Gap Risk

↔ Technical §5.1 · Numba State-Machine Loop & Telemetry Schema

The theory above is written in continuous time; the data arrives as one-minute bars that record only the open, high, low and close. Something must be assumed about the order in which things happened inside a bar, and every assumption is made in the direction that is unfavourable to the strategy.

The order of a bar

  1. The trigger comes first. $P_{\text{floor},t}$ is computed from the state at the end of the previous bar. If $L_t \le P_{\text{floor},t}$ the emergency exit fires at $P_{\text{trig}} = \min(O_t, P_{\text{floor},t})$, any resting order is cancelled, and nothing else can change the position in a way that would have avoided it.
  2. Then the resting maker order is resolved against the same bar with the liquidity model of §3.1.
  3. Then the envelope is evaluated at the close and any new order is routed. Its taker part executes at the close; its maker part waits for the next bar.

Decisions use only information available at the close; fills use only the bar that follows. There is no look-ahead.

A purchase and the rest of its bar

A resting bid fills when the market trades down to it, so the low of the bar comes at or after the fill. The purchase raises the trigger (§1.2). If the low of that same bar is at or below the new trigger, a continuous market crossed it after the fill and the exit must be taken in that bar, at the new trigger. The model does exactly that. The rebuy cap guarantees the new trigger is below the purchase price, so this exit is itself covered by Proposition 1.

The floor invariant

Proposition 3 · $V_t \ge F$ on every bar that does not gap

Assume $V_{t-1} \ge F$ at the previous close and $O_t \ge P_{\text{floor},t}$. Then $V_t \ge F$ at the close of bar $t$.

Proof by cases.

  • Trigger reached. The exit is executed at $P_{\text{floor},t}$ and Proposition 1 gives $c_t \ge F$.
  • Trigger not reached, no trade. $C_t \ge L_t > P_{\text{floor},t} \ge (F-c)/(n(1-\gamma))$, which is $V_t > F$.
  • Maker sale fills. With $f_{\text{maker}} < \gamma$ its net price is at least $A/n$, so by the lemma of §1.2 the required level does not rise and the previous case applies to the new state.
  • Taker sale at the close. It nets exactly $C_t(1-\gamma)$ per coin, the value $V_t$ already assigned to it: $V_t$ is unchanged.
  • Purchase, taker or maker. The rebuy cap leaves $P > P_{\text{floor}}'$ at the price of the purchase. For a taker buy that price is $C_t$. For a maker buy either $L_t > P_{\text{floor}}'$, and then $C_t \ge L_t$ is above it too, or the exit is taken at $P_{\text{floor}}'$ as described above. ∎

By induction from $V_0 \ge F$, which holds whenever the window starts above the trigger, the floor holds on every bar up to the first gap. The model checks it on every bar with a tolerance of $10^{-6}$ USD for floating-point rounding and reports the count on the Floor Integrity card.

Gap risk

If a bar opens below the trigger, the market never traded at the trigger and no order could have. The exit is executed at the open, and the account ends below the floor by

$$F - c_t \;\le\; n_{t-1}\,\big(P_{\text{floor},t} - O_t\big)\,(1-\gamma).$$

No trading rule can remove this; only holding less can reduce it, which is what a higher $\rho$, a larger buffer or a wider band do. On liquid one-minute Bitcoin data a gap of more than the buffer between two consecutive bars is rare, but it is the event the buffer does not cover, and the workbench labels it GAP BREACH rather than hiding it. Starting a back-test with the first price already below the trigger is the same event at bar zero.

§6.1Reading the Experiment: Market Data, Rescaling, KPIs & the Monte Carlo Fan

↔ Technical §6.1 · Data Engine, Recalculation Pipeline & UI Event Bindings

What the bundled data is

The bundled dataset is the real one-minute history of the Binance spot pair BTCUSDT from January 2023 to October 2026. It carries, for every minute, the open, high, low and close and the volume initiated by buyers and by sellers separately; the flow imbalance of §4.1 and the absorption factor of §3.1 are computed from recorded aggression, not inferred from candle shapes. Two caveats belong with it.

  • The quote currency is the dollar stablecoin USDT, used as a proxy for USD. The two normally trade within a few basis points of each other.
  • Minutes during which the exchange was down are absent rather than filled in. The model treats the bars on either side as consecutive, which makes such a gap a candidate for the gap event of §5.1.

Imported candles without a taker split receive one synthesised from the close location value (Technical §4.1), and the dataset selector says so.

What price rescaling preserves

Historical crashes happened at many price levels; the floor of a present-day account is at one. Normalize Start Price to Reference Anchor multiplies every price of the window by $\kappa = P_{\text{anchor}} / O_0$. Returns $P_{t+1}/P_t$ are untouched, and with them the shape of the path, its volatility clustering and, since volumes are not rescaled, the flow imbalance. What is not scale-free are the model's absolute dollar constants: the 1 USD floor on the ATR and the 250 USD near-floor zone. A window rescaled by a factor far from one is a stress test with a realistic shape, not a replay of history, and the amber chip in the top bar keeps that visible.

What the fan does and does not say

The fan holds prices, signals and parameters fixed and varies only the uniform variates of the fill model. It answers "how much of this result was luck in the queue?" It does not model alternative price paths; for that, change the window. A narrow fan means the outcome is determined by the market and the parameters; a wide one means the strategy leans on passive fills at moments when they are uncertain, and a higher $\rho$ will trade some of that width for certainty.

A window with no orders

With the default floor of 60,000 and reference of 72,000 the default window, January 2026, never trades: Bitcoin spent that month between roughly 75,700 and 97,900, always above the reference, where $d_t = 1$, the ceiling equals $n_{\max}$ and the envelope has nothing to ask for. That is the correct answer, and the chart says so. To see the machinery work, rescale the window to start near the floor or load one of the scenarios in the top bar, several of which are 2026 periods that test the default floor at real prices.
